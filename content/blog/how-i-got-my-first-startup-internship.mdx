I got an internship at a startup that had raised about $2 million without any traditional job experience.

There wasn't a secret referral. I didn't have a polished résumé full of internships. I sent an email that was direct, specific, and a little scary to press send on.

This is what I wrote, what I think made it work, and what I'd do again.

## The situation

I had built things, but I hadn't been hired to build things. That's an important distinction: no company had given me a title, but I had spent years learning by making websites and small products.

So I stopped treating “no experience” as the whole story. My experience was simply not formal yet.

I chose a startup whose product I genuinely cared about. They were solving a problem I understood: applying to jobs is exhausting, repetitive, and full of tiny bits of friction. I already had ideas about what could make that experience better.

That gave me something more useful than a generic application: a real reason to write.

It wasn't perfect. If I sent it today, I would tighten it significantly. But it was honest, and it made a concrete case for why I was writing that person, not just any person with an inbox.

## What I think made it work

### I led with proof, not labels

I couldn't say “I have three years of professional experience.” So I showed what I had actually made. Personal projects are evidence that you can notice a problem, make decisions, and ship something that exists outside your laptop.

The bar isn't “build the next huge company.” Build things that are real enough to explain: who they are for, what problem they solve, what you learned, and what you would change.

### I had a reason to care about *that* company

“I love your mission” is easy to ignore. Specific product thoughts are harder to ignore.

I mentioned areas I had been thinking about: interview prep, personalisation, and small product improvements. They proved I had spent time with the problem. I wasn't asking for a job in the abstract. I was offering to think alongside the team.

### I made the ask unambiguous

The email says what I wanted: an internship, a chance to learn, and a few ways I could contribute. Whoever reads it should not have to infer the request from a long story.

Asking to work for free was not the important part. The important part was lowering the perceived risk: I was ready to learn, contribute, and prove myself through the work.

### I sent it before I felt ready

There is no version of your first cold email where you feel fully qualified. Waiting for one more project, one more credential, or the perfect portfolio can become a very effective way to avoid being rejected.

I sent the message with no guarantee of a reply. That was the whole move.

## What I'd change next time

The original email tried to say too much. A stronger version would be shorter, put the most relevant project first, and end with one easy next step: a 15-minute conversation.

It would look more like this:

> Hi [Name]. I build [brief description of the kind of thing you build], and I've been following how [Company] tackles [specific problem].
>
> I made [one relevant project], where I learned [one concrete skill or outcome]. I also have a few ideas about [specific product area] that I'd be excited to explore with your team.
>
> I'm looking for an internship where I can contribute in [one or two areas]. Would you be open to a short conversation next week?

Shorter makes every sentence earn its place.

## The real takeaway

No experience does not mean nothing to offer. It means you have to make your evidence visible yourself.

Build things. Write down what you notice. Reach out to teams working on problems you care about. Make a clear ask. Then send it before you've managed to talk yourself out of it.

That email got me my first opportunity. The work after it is what will make it count.
